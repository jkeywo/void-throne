How to read this

Each run has five distinct player agents and one facilitator agent. All five player personalities change between runs. Conversations are separated by phase and by table; mechanical records have a green background. Locked order sheets sit side by side after each faction and council phase. The resolution compares the opening and closing state.

This is an agent playtest of a paper game. The printed minute labels show the proposed 60-minute schedule; agents did not play in real time. Role agents ran in batches within the available concurrency, with bounded message exchanges. Private knowledge was passed through explicit packets. This can expose design problems and examples of bargaining; it does not establish human comprehension, session length or balance.

Assets, Charter codes and the common starting position

Wayfarer is the Guild freighter; Lantern is its survey tender and navigation team. Spear is the Janissariat field cohort; Vigil is its garrison. Grey Petrel is the Ascendant’s one-use reserve cutter. The Narrow is the threatened route; Beacon measures its installation, Communities local stores and confidence, and Readiness conditioned military power.

The assets make the negotiation easier to specify

Players could point to Wayfarer, Lantern, Spear, Vigil and Grey Petrel when explaining a promise or refusal. Wayfarer could trade or carry council cargo; Lantern could maintain the beacon or support its single council action; Vigil could serve a Warrant or Guard, never both. Two vessels crossing meant two separate wear costs. Agents identified these relationships in their own reasoning and debriefs. This is useful evidence of clarity within the transcripts, not a measured comparison of human comprehension against the earlier token system.

Compromise is real, but the burdens are uneven

Run one obtained a settlement with a missed Guild contract, military scrutiny and substantial imperial delegation. Run two preserved both contracts but failed ratification. Run three kept both contracts and ratified the Guild's preferred package: the Guild's concession was a continuing upkeep duty, while the crown spent its reserve and lost both independent powers. The goal of making nearly everyone give something up is met more clearly through legal obligations than immediate material losses; these runs do not establish balanced sacrifice. A promise on an unsuccessful Charter is not a cost paid, and civilian suffering is not a sacrifice by the player ordering the Raid.

A quiet final month can be a small sacrifice

Readiness falling from 4 to 3 is a real counter loss, but both values have the same strong-support ending and meet the Readiness requirement for a Warrant where the Charter permits one. All five run-three players described that reduction as cheap in debrief. Early combat therefore buys an inexpensive quiet month at the end of this two-round scenario. By contrast, falling from 3 to 2 in run one removed a live imperial option. Do not count those two reductions as equally costly, or treat giving up a hoped-for autonomy article as losing a power the army already possessed.

The army's condition changes what the Ascendant can do

Runs one and two both ended at Readiness 3, but reached it differently. A peaceful opening in run one reduced Readiness to 2 and removed the next month's Warrant option. Early fighting in run two raised Readiness to 4, preserving that option in round two. The mechanism therefore gave the Ascendant a concrete reason to want a fighting army even while bargaining over a better government. In run three, the humanitarian commander explicitly accepted decline and opposed speculative violence; the Ascendant used offered Spear anyway under Interim authority. Operational coordination should not be described as unanimous political cooperation.

Foresight and unilateral choices had different levels of exposure

The Security visions were useful when they connected a live Raid choice to an actual voyage: run one coordinated Relief and Raid, and run two chose a productive first Raid then avoided an empty second one. Run three's second vision confirmed a repair plan after Spear had already been withheld. No Ascendant selected the second Navigation vision; its Repair/Trade benefit still applied in runs two and three. Private Trade was exercised repeatedly, and run three demonstrated imperial use of offered Spear over a delegate's objection, followed by the faction physically withholding it. No faction chose independent Raid; no Shared passage, Military/Campaign operation, permanent asset loss or triggered Tender cover occurred. Those branches remain behaviourally untested.

The constitutional experiment did not happen

No round-one council obtained matching article signatures in any of the three runs. Every round-two operation therefore used Interim authority. Players changed offers after seeing resource costs and the existing command arrangement, but none experienced a newly delegated power in operation. This is the clearest gap in the evidence. The bounded agent exchanges may have contributed: run one included late-delivered offers, and run three did not relay every addressed closing reply back before resolution. The games were not played against a human five- or seven-minute clock. Differences between runs cannot be attributed to personality alone, and this does not establish that live councils would fail to agree.

What the next paper test should check

Keep the named cards and use a brief spoken readback at each bell: asset, assignment, authorised use, and reserve trigger. Put the exact proposed Charter option and its three signatures in the centre of each council. Observe whether people can reach a provisional agreement with live conversation; if they repeatedly cannot, test moving that agreement deadline to the end of round-one free discussion in a later draft. Also allow a short faction discussion before the final replacement package. None of those changes was introduced during these v0.2 simulations.

Concrete assets

Wayfarer could Trade or carry the army; it could not do both. Lantern's Maintain assignment was equally explicit. The Guild's requested Grey Petrel Relief lift could not replace the Ascendant's already lodged Tender cover. These distinctions made the mismatch visible. They did not prevent it.

Actual compromise

The Guild completed one contract and then sacrificed the second to provide Relief. Readiness fell from 3 to 2 during restraint, removing the next month's Warrant option, before a Raid restored it to 3. The final N-G/M-J settlement places upkeep on the Guild, military operations under both delegates, and both council powers outside the Ascendant's sole control. The Raid caused two points of civilian harm; final Communities 2 is a cost borne by people outside the player factions.

Foresight

The first Navigation warning did not affect the eventual Hold order; Tender cover returned unused. The second, selected Security vision identified the delivered-Relief condition, allowing a funded Relief-and-Raid plan. Grey Petrel remained unused throughout, and could not substitute for military transport or the Guild's real concession.

Constitutional learning

The army moved from N-J/M-A to accepting N-G/M-J; the Ascendant moved from N-G/M-I to N-G/M-J. Both Guild players started and ended favouring N-G/M-J, although their intermediate M-I offers and willingness to support fighting changed. Ratification should not be counted as a change of preference for every player.

What was not tested

Neither provisional article received matching signatures in round one. Round two therefore still used Interim powers: this run does not test players living under new provisional authority. Some first-round requests and a revised offer reached recipients only after their relevant commitments or final recommendations; the bounded message sequence contributed to the deadlock and is not evidence that a live paper table would necessarily fail in the same way.
